Mapping AI's Infrastructures

Companies: Google & Microsoft · Platform: Google My Maps · 17 locations · 3 layers · Week 7

🔵 Blue — Headquarters  |  🟢 Green — Data Centers  |  🔴 Red — Data Labor  ·  Open full map →

The Map

This map visualizes the global infrastructure behind artificial intelligence systems developed by Google and Microsoft. The map contains 17 locations across three layers to reveal how power, infrastructure, and labor are geographically distributed in AI development.

The first layer (blue markers) represents corporate headquarters, including Google in Mountain View and Microsoft in Redmond. Their concentration in Silicon Valley and major U.S. technology hubs shows how decision-making power in AI remains geographically centralized.

The second layer (green markers) represents data centers — the physical infrastructure that powers cloud computing and AI systems. These facilities are located in Iowa, Oregon, Finland, and Belgium, chosen for cheap land, reliable electricity, renewable energy sources, and government tax incentives. Data centers reveal the material and environmental footprint behind technologies often described as "the cloud."

The third layer (red markers) represents data labeling and moderation labor, including locations in Manila, Nairobi, Bangalore, Hyderabad, and Mexico City. Workers in these regions label datasets, review harmful online content, and evaluate AI responses. Their work is essential for training machine learning systems and making platforms safe, yet it is often invisible in discussions about AI.

Research Documentation

To research the locations included in this map, I used a combination of academic sources, investigative journalism, and company infrastructure documentation. A key source was Kate Crawford's Atlas of AI, which examines the environmental and labor systems that support artificial intelligence technologies. Crawford's work helped frame AI as a global infrastructure rather than simply software or algorithms.

I also consulted reports from the AI Now Institute, which studies the social and political impacts of AI systems. Their research helped explain how AI development depends on large networks of human labor, particularly data labeling and content moderation. For information about data center locations, I used company infrastructure pages from Google and Microsoft, along with environmental reports such as Greenpeace's Click Clean project, which investigates the energy use of cloud computing.

Investigative journalism from Rest of World, The Guardian, and Time Magazine was especially helpful for identifying locations where AI training labor takes place. These sources describe how workers in countries such as Kenya, India, and the Philippines perform tasks like labeling images, reviewing harmful online content, and evaluating machine learning outputs.

Artist Statement

This map argues that artificial intelligence is not simply a digital or virtual technology but a global system shaped by power, geography, and labor. By mapping headquarters, data centers, and data labeling labor associated with Google and Microsoft, the visualization reveals how the benefits and costs of AI are unevenly distributed across different regions.

A clear pattern emerges when these layers are viewed together. Decision-making power is concentrated in a few technology hubs in the United States, where companies control the development and governance of AI systems. Meanwhile, the infrastructure required to operate AI is distributed across regions where electricity is cheaper, renewable energy is available, or governments offer incentives. And the human labor that trains and moderates AI systems is frequently outsourced to workers in the Philippines, Kenya, and India — whose work is essential but often invisible in narratives that focus only on technological innovation.

This project connects to ideas from D'Ignazio and Klein's Data Feminism, particularly the importance of making hidden systems of power visible through data visualization. Instead of mapping only innovation or corporate success, this map acts as a form of counter-mapping that highlights the infrastructures and labor that sustain AI technologies.

Reflection

When looking at the map, the first thing that stands out is the geographic concentration of corporate headquarters in the United States. While Google and Microsoft operate globally, the centers of decision-making remain in a few powerful technology hubs. At the same time, data centers appear more geographically dispersed, often located in places with cheaper energy or favorable policies.

What is hardest to see on the map is the full scale of the human labor involved in AI systems. Data labeling and moderation work are often outsourced through contractors, and companies rarely publish exact locations or workforce numbers. This makes it difficult to fully map the global workforce that supports AI technologies.

The map also raises several important questions. Who benefits economically from AI development, and who bears the environmental and labor costs? Why are technology companies transparent about data centers but less transparent about outsourced labor? And how might the geography of AI change if governments begin regulating energy use, labor conditions, or data governance more strictly? These questions suggest that AI is not only a technical system but also a political and economic one shaped by global inequalities.
