Mapping AI's Infrastructures

Platform: Google My Maps · 17 locations · 3 layers · Week 7

About the Map

This map visualizes the global infrastructure behind AI systems developed by Google and Microsoft. It contains 17 locations across three layers to reveal how power, infrastructure, and labor are geographically distributed in AI development.

🔵 Blue — Headquarters (Mountain View, Redmond): Where major decisions about AI strategy, investment, and governance are made. Decision-making power in AI is geographically centralized in a handful of US technology hubs.

🟢 Green — Data Centers (Iowa, Oregon, Finland, Belgium): The physical infrastructure required to power cloud computing and AI. These locations are chosen for cheap land, access to renewable energy, reliable electricity, and government tax incentives — revealing AI's environmental footprint.

🔴 Red — Data Labor (Manila, Nairobi, Bangalore, Hyderabad, Mexico City): Workers who label images, review harmful content, and evaluate AI outputs. Their work is necessary to train machine learning systems, yet it is rarely visible to users.

Artist Statement

This map argues that artificial intelligence is not simply a digital or virtual technology but a global system shaped by power, geography, and labor. A clear pattern emerges when the layers are viewed together: decision-making power is concentrated in a few US technology hubs, while infrastructure is distributed to regions with cheaper energy, and human labor is outsourced to workers in the Philippines, Kenya, and India.

This project connects to ideas from D'Ignazio and Klein's Data Feminism — specifically, the importance of making hidden systems of power visible through data visualization. Instead of mapping only innovation or corporate success, this map acts as a form of counter-mapping that highlights the infrastructures and labor that sustain AI technologies.

Reflection

What is hardest to see on the map is the full scale of the human labor involved in AI systems. Data labeling and moderation work are often outsourced through contractors, and companies rarely publish exact locations or workforce numbers. This makes it difficult to fully map the global workforce that supports AI technologies.

What surprised me most was how distributed the AI system actually is. While public narratives about AI often focus on Silicon Valley innovation, the infrastructure spans rural regions, global labor markets, and energy systems. Mapping these locations revealed how AI is supported by a complex global network that extends far beyond the technology companies themselves — and how unevenly the benefits and costs are distributed.
