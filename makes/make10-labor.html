The Hidden Workers of AI

Tools: ChatGPT, Python/ReportLab · Week 11

⚙️

Design Statement

I chose the infographic format over a zine because my primary audience is classmates - people who use ChatGPT regularly but have likely never thought about who built it. An infographic communicates key facts quickly, in a format that is easy to share digitally, screenshot, or reference in conversation. A zine requires more sustained reading; an infographic can land its argument in under two minutes.

The color palette - black, white, and blue - was a deliberate choice. Blue is used exclusively for elements representing institutional power: section labels, the AI company column, and the dominant bars in the wage chart. Gray and neutral tones represent workers and their conditions. This color logic encodes the power relationship without stating it explicitly - blue accumulates at the top of every section, mirroring how money and power accumulate at the top of the supply chain.

The wage comparison bar chart is the visual anchor: $12.50 per hour billed to OpenAI vs. $1.32 per hour taken home by workers in Kenya. I wanted one image that communicated the core argument at a glance. I want the audience to leave with one number they cannot forget.

Research Notes

The primary source was TIME Magazine's investigation by Billy Perrigo (January 2023): "OpenAI Used Kenyan Workers on Less Than $2 Per Hour." It included workers' payslips, internal Sama and OpenAI documents, and direct interviews. It provided the core wage data ($1.32-$2.00/hr take-home vs. $12.50/hr billed to OpenAI) and documented the psychological conditions workers faced.

Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri's Ghost Work (2019) gave theoretical grounding for the "ghost work" phenomenon - the structural invisibility of gig-based AI labor platforms. Their framework helped explain why this labor disappears from public view by design, not by accident.

Reflection

After researching this topic, I was genuinely surprised by how much human labor goes into something we think of as purely technological. The "invisible workers" of AI are mostly gig workers in countries like Kenya, India, and the Philippines who spend their days labeling data, filtering graphic content, and ranking AI responses - work that is essential but completely hidden from the public.

What struck me most was how deliberate that invisibility is. OpenAI did not accidentally underpay workers - they routed labor through a contractor called Sama, which created distance between the company and the people actually doing the work. Workers took home as little as $1.32 an hour while OpenAI billed $12.50. That gap does not happen by mistake. The system was designed so that exploitation would be invisible. That intentionality was more disturbing than the wage number alone.

Hicks's idea of the "cis-tem" clicked for me here. The system is not broken - it is working exactly as designed, just for some people and not others. That was the most uncomfortable takeaway from this project.
