Sustainability & Ethics

Every website has a carbon cost. Every AI query has one too. This page documents the steps I took to reduce this portfolio's footprint — and what I learned from reckoning with AI's environmental impact.

The Reading That Changed How I Think About This

Crawford and Joler's "Anatomy of an AI System" (2018) traces the full material stack of a single Amazon Echo voice command — from lithium mines in Bolivia to server farms in Virginia to the underpaid click-workers who labeled training data. The piece makes viscerally clear that AI is not immaterial. It runs on land, water, energy, and human bodies.

Tomlinson et al.'s piece on the carbon emissions of AI writing offered a more complicated counterpoint: in some narrow contexts, AI-assisted writing may produce fewer emissions than human writing alone. But this framing risks becoming a justification for uncritical use — efficiency arguments tend to obscure questions of necessity and distribution. Just because something is more efficient does not mean we should do more of it.

Building Make 11 (AI & Ecology) forced me to confront numbers I had been comfortable ignoring. A single GPT-4 query uses roughly the energy equivalent of charging a smartphone for several minutes. Multiplied across billions of queries a day, the scale is staggering — and growing.

Concrete Steps I Took

Here are the specific actions I took to reduce the environmental footprint of this site:

Compressed All Images

Every image in this portfolio was compressed before upload using Squoosh or similar tools. Uncompressed images are one of the largest contributors to page weight and therefore to data transfer energy costs.

Result: Images are served at the minimum quality necessary for the viewing context — thumbnails at ~80px wide are not sent as 3000px originals.

No Tracking Scripts or Analytics

This site uses no third-party trackers, analytics scripts, or advertising tags. Many portfolio sites quietly load dozens of tracking scripts that run on every page load, consuming energy and sending data to third parties without the visitor's knowledge.

Result: The only JavaScript on this site is the small inline mobile nav toggle — nothing external is loaded.

Limited External Embeds

Where possible, I used screenshots or static images instead of live embeds (e.g., for maps, Voyant visualizations, and social content). Live embeds load third-party scripts on every visit, multiplying the energy cost per page view.

Result: Embeds are used only where a static image genuinely cannot convey the artifact's interactivity.

Static HTML — No Database or Server Processing

This entire site is static HTML and CSS — no server-side processing, no database queries, no backend. GitHub Pages serves pre-built files directly, which is dramatically more energy-efficient than dynamic sites that generate pages on demand.

Result: Every page load is a simple file transfer, not a computation.

System Fonts Considered

This site does load two external font families from Google Fonts (Playfair Display and DM Sans). This is a deliberate trade-off: typography is central to the site's legibility and design argument. I chose fonts served by Google's CDN, which uses renewable energy, and limited the number of weights loaded to the minimum needed.

Mindful AI Use

Knowing that each AI query has an energy cost changed how I used these tools. I batched related questions rather than sending dozens of short queries, avoided regenerating outputs when the first was usable, and tried to use AI deliberately rather than reflexively.

Reflection: The Ethics of Using AI to Study AI

There is an obvious tension in a course about AI's costs that requires us to use AI tools. I do not think the answer is to refuse to use them — that would be both impractical and intellectually dishonest about the world we actually live in. But Crawford and Joler's work convinced me that awareness matters: using AI without acknowledging its costs is a form of willful blindness that benefits the companies who profit from that invisibility.

The steps above are small. They do not offset the AI queries I sent this semester, and they do not address the structural drivers of AI's environmental impact — decisions made by corporations and governments, not individual students. But they are honest. They represent a choice to see the cost, name it, and do what I can within my sphere of control — which is, I think, what critical making asks of us.
