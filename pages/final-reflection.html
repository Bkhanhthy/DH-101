Final Reflection · DH 101 · Spring 2025

What AI Cannot Replace

On identity, judgment, and the irreducibly human in an age of generative machines.

Introduction: The Flattening

When I enrolled in DH 101, I thought I understood what AI was: a tool, a shortcut, a convenience. By the end of the semester — after twelve makes, twelve reflections, and a lot of failed prompts — I understand something different. AI is not just a tool. It is a way of seeing. And the way it sees is flat.

My central argument is this: AI systems are dangerous not because they are too powerful, but because they are too convincing at appearing to understand us — when in fact they are averaging us. They flatten identity into statistical patterns, compress human judgment into probability distributions, and make the extraordinary look ordinary. But my making process this semester showed me that human creativity and human judgment are not just different from what AI does — they are fundamentally irreplaceable by it.

The Stochastic Parrot and the Averaged Self

The reading that stayed with me longest was Bender et al.'s "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" (2021). The authors argue that large language models do not understand language — they predict statistically likely sequences of words based on patterns in training data. This sounds abstract until you experience it firsthand.

"The question is not whether these models understand, but whether we do — whether we understand what we are asking of them, and what they are actually doing when they respond." — paraphrased from Bender et al., 2021

When I made my AI selfie (Make 2), I gave DALL·E a prompt describing myself. The image it returned was technically competent and entirely wrong. It had averaged me — produced the most statistically likely version of someone matching my description, smoothing out everything specific about me in favor of what "usually" looks like that. This is exactly what Bender et al. warn about: the model does not see me, it sees the pattern I approximate.

Data, Power, and Who Gets Erased

D'Ignazio and Klein's Data Feminism (2020) gave me a different lens on the same problem. They argue that data is never neutral — it reflects the priorities, biases, and power structures of the people and institutions that collected it. When I built my map of AI infrastructures (Make 6), I was shocked by how literally true this is: the physical infrastructure of AI — data centers, mining operations, server farms — is geographically concentrated in ways that mirror colonial patterns of resource extraction.

D'Ignazio and Klein ask us to look at who benefits and who bears the cost. In AI, the answer is almost always the same: the benefits flow to wealthy institutions and tech companies in the Global North, while the environmental and labor costs fall on communities with the least power to resist. My map made this visible in a way that just reading about it did not.

The Anatomy of a Make: Where Judgment Lives

Crawford and Joler's "Anatomy of an AI System" (2018) traces the full supply chain of a single Amazon Echo command — from the rare earth minerals in its hardware to the human labelers who trained its models. The piece is a reminder that every AI interaction is built on layers of invisible human labor and planetary cost.

This stayed with me across every make I did this semester. When I used ChatGPT to help draft prompts, or when I used Canva's AI tools to generate imagery, I was drawing on that same hidden stack. But here is what I also found: in every case, the AI did the generation, and I did the judgment. I decided what was good. I decided what was honest. I decided what was meaningful. No matter how much the machine produced, it never decided anything.

What AI Cannot Do

After twelve makes, I can say this with confidence: AI can generate. It cannot judge. It can produce infinite options. It cannot care which one is right. It can pattern-match identity. It cannot know a person.

The most important moments in my making this semester were not the moments when AI surprised me with something good. They were the moments when AI failed — when it averaged, when it hallucinated, when it produced something technically correct and humanly wrong — and I had to decide what to do about it. Those moments of failure and correction were where I found the most interesting thinking.

Ratto's framework of "critical making" (2011) names what I was doing without knowing it: using the act of making as a form of inquiry. Each artifact was not just a product — it was a question. What can a machine do here? What can't it? Where does it stop and I begin?

Conclusion

AI is not going to stop flattening. The systems are getting better at producing convincing outputs, which makes it more important, not less, to practice the kind of critical judgment this course asked of us. The question is not whether to use AI — we already do, in ways we rarely notice. The question is whether we use it with eyes open: knowing what it can and cannot do, knowing whose labor and whose land it runs on, and knowing that the judgment — the meaning-making — remains irreducibly ours.

I started this semester thinking AI was a shortcut. I am ending it understanding that the shortcut is also a choice — and that every choice about how we use these tools is a choice about what kind of humans we want to be.
